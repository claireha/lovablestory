The Lookalike

Start from
something real.

Before you can prototype a change, you need a realistic base to change. I asked Lovable to recreate HubSpot's chatflow step editor from a plain-language description — left sidebar, center canvas, right configuration panel.

One prompt. 90 seconds. A clickable mock that actually looked like our product.

The key skill: be specific about layout before anything else. Name the panels, name the visual style, reference real product context.

The aha moment

"I'd been describing UIs in words for years. The moment I could show someone instead of explain — the conversation changed completely. In 10 minutes we uncovered alignment gaps that would have taken 3 review cycles."

02
Add a Feature

One prompt.
One change.

Once you have a realistic base, adding a feature is fast. I added a "Route to Agent" step type — described what the panel should show, what options it needed, and what the fallback behavior looked like.

The constraint that matters: one specific thing per prompt. Scope tight. Three things at once gets muddy output — and muddy output slows the conversation you're trying to start.

I showed this to my TL before writing a single line of the spec.

The aha moment

"We caught two edge cases in the first 5 minutes of clicking. Not because we were looking for them — but because clicking a thing surfaces friction that reading about a thing never does."

03
Dogfood Your Product

Feel the
friction yourself.

The most underrated use: build a customer-facing flow and click through it yourself — as if you're the customer. I built a full chat widget experience. Greeting, branching question, three clickable options. Then I used it.

The mindset shift is everything. You're not designing for a review. You're simulating an experience. Write the prompt from the customer's perspective, not the PM's.

You find friction faster by using a prototype than by reading a spec.

The aha moment

"I caught three things I would never have caught in a doc. The third option in the flow felt wrong the moment I clicked it. No one told me — I just felt it. I knew exactly what to fix."
